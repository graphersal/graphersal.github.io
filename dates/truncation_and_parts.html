Truncation and Parts

date_trunc(DT.unit) is PostgreSQL's date_trunc, and date_part(DT.unit) is its extract. Both are Graphersal extensions; TinkerPop has neither. GQL's X.truncate() and x.year do the same with Neo4j's names, but truncate millennia and centuries as Neo4j does (Dates in GQL). A datetime is read on its own wall clock, or on the wall clock of a zone given as the last argument (Time Zones).

date_trunc()

The fields below the unit become their first value: the start of the hour, the day, the month. A truncated datetime keeps its offset (or, with a zone, takes the zone's offset at the new instant, and the zone's name when it is an IANA zone).

g.inject(datetime("2023-08-17T15:47:12.5+02:00")).date_trunc(DT.hour).next()   // datetime("2023-08-17T15:00:00+02:00")
g.inject(datetime("2023-08-17T15:47:12.5+02:00")).date_trunc(DT.week).next()   // datetime("2023-08-14T00:00:00+02:00")
g.inject(datetime("2023-08-17T15:47:12.5+02:00")).date_trunc(DT.day, "UTC").next()   // datetime("2023-08-17T00:00:00Z")
g.inject(date("2023-08-17")).date_trunc(DT.quarter).next()   // date("2023-07-01")
g.inject(duration("P1Y2M3DT4H5M")).date_trunc(DT.day).next()   // duration("P1Y2M3D")
UnitTruncates to
nanosecond ... hourthe start of that unit of the clock (nanosecond keeps the value)
daymidnight
weekMonday, midnight (ISO 8601 weeks)
month, quarter, yearthe first day of the month, of the quarter (January, April, July, October), of the year
decadethe first day of a year divisible by 10 (2020-01-01)
century, millenniumthe first day of a year ending in 01/001: centuries and millennia start at year 1, as PostgreSQL counts them (2001-01-01 starts the 21st century)

A date takes day and larger, a time and an offsettime hour and smaller (the offset stays), a duration keeps the fields at and above the unit (month and larger keep whole multiples of the unit's months and drop the days and the time part).

date_part()

One field as a number: an int64, except DT.epoch, a float64 of seconds.

PartValue
DT.year, DT.month (1-12), DT.day (1-31), DT.hour, DT.minute, DT.second (0-59)the field
DT.millisecond (0-999), DT.microsecond (0-999999), DT.nanosecond (0-999999999)the fraction of the second in that unit; unlike PostgreSQL, not the seconds with the fraction
DT.quarter (1-4)the quarter
DT.week (1-53)the ISO 8601 week number
DT.iso_yearthe ISO 8601 week-numbering year (2023-01-01 is in week 52 of 2022)
DT.day_of_week0 = Sunday to 6 = Saturday (PostgreSQL dow)
DT.iso_day_of_week1 = Monday to 7 = Sunday (PostgreSQL isodow)
DT.day_of_year (1-366)the day of the year
DT.decade, DT.century, DT.millenniumas PostgreSQL counts them (the 21st century is 2001-2100)
DT.epochseconds since 1970-01-01T00:00:00Z (a localdatetime read as UTC); for a duration its length with a year of 365.25 days and a month of 30 days
DT.offsetthe UTC offset of a datetime or an offsettime in seconds
g.inject(date("2023-08-17")).date_part(DT.day_of_week).next()   // 4
g.inject(datetime("2023-01-01T15:47:12.5+02:00")).date_part(DT.week).next()   // 52
g.inject(datetime("2023-01-01T15:47:12.5+02:00")).date_part(DT.iso_year).next()   // 2022
g.inject(datetime("2023-08-17T15:47:12.5+02:00")).date_part(DT.epoch).next()   // 1692280032.5
g.inject(datetime("2023-08-17T15:47:12.5+02:00")).date_part(DT.offset).next()   // 7200
g.inject(datetime("2023-08-02T02:00Z")).date_part(DT.day, "America/New_York").next()   // 1

A date has no clock parts (hour and smaller), a time only those (and epoch, the seconds since midnight). A duration gives PostgreSQL's interval fields: year and month from its months (14 months are 1 year and 2 months), day its days, hour and smaller from its time part; it has no week, day-of parts, iso_year or offset:

g.inject(duration("P1Y2M3DT4H5M6.5S")).date_part(DT.month).next()   // 2
g.inject(date("2023-08-17")).date_part(DT.hour).next()   // error: the part hour is not defined for a date
